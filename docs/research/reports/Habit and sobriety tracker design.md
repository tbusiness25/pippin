# Count the alcohol-free days kept, not lost

Pippin should build its habit tracker and its sobriety tracker on the same idea: **progress accumulates and a slip is information, not a reset**. For habits, that means an if-then anchor on every habit, flexible "X times a week" targets and a Loop-style habit-strength score that a single miss dents by about 5%. Pippin's optional streaks can stay alongside, as long as they are forgiving and secondary. For sobriety, the headline number should be **alcohol-free days this year**, which can never go down, with "days since last drink" offered as a secondary, opt-in run. A drink is logged as a lapse event that leads into a short problem-solving debrief, never as a "reset" button. This layout follows the best-supported relapse model: Marlatt's abstinence violation effect, where reading one drink as total failure is what turns a lapse into a relapse. It also follows the evidence on digital alcohol interventions, which work modestly (**about 3 UK units a week** on average) and get their effect from self-monitoring, behaviour substitution and problem solving, not from clocks, badges or savings counters. Safety comes before day one. A UK AUDIT-C screen runs at onboarding. A score of **5 or more** opens the full AUDIT. A score of **20 or more**, or any history of morning drinking, shakes or fits, shows a "don't stop suddenly, see your GP first" screen, and emergency withdrawal guidance stays one tap away for the first five days. For a medicated user with ADHD, the year is also a medication decision. ADHD raises the risk of alcohol problems, and stimulant treatment is associated with about a third fewer substance-related events. Alcohol combined with lisdexamfetamine adds cardiovascular strain, so staying on medication and staying alcohol-free support each other. For a public release that tracks other substances and behaviours, the same counter becomes dangerous in some categories: abrupt stopping can kill with benzodiazepines, and a lapse after abstinence can be fatal with opioids. Each category therefore needs its own safety gate, its own helpline and its own language.

## Habits take about two months to form, and one missed day barely registers

The habit tracker should model the curve that people actually follow, not the 21-day myth. In Lally's field study, automaticity rose along an asymptotic curve and plateaued after an average of **66 days, with a range of 18 to 254** ([Gardner, Lally & Wardle 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3553622/)). A 2024 meta-analysis of **20 studies and 2,601 participants** found a median of 59–66 days, a mean of 106–154 days, and individual times from 4 to 335 days. It also found that morning practice, self-chosen behaviours, enjoyment and specific time-and-place planning all strengthened habits. The analysis is weak in places: 11 of the 20 studies were at high risk of bias and heterogeneity was 87% ([Singh et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/)). The most useful single finding for a no-shame design is Lally's: "missing the occasional opportunity to perform the behaviour did not seriously impair the habit formation process" ([The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit)). A popular claim that two or three consecutive misses "slow the curve" could not be traced to the primary paper and should not be repeated. Once formed, habits are triggered by recurring context cues and become relatively **insensitive to reward** ([Wood & Rünger 2016](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.runger.2016.pdf)). So Pippin's pet and rewards matter most during the formation months and can safely matter less afterwards.

Of the planning techniques, implementation intentions have the strongest evidence. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis of **94 studies and more than 8,000 participants** found an effect of **d = 0.65** on goal attainment ([Uni Konstanz](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)). "After I [existing routine], I will [tiny version]" is the same mechanism as habit stacking, so it should be the default shape of every new habit. Temptation bundling, where an audiobook could only be heard at the gym, raised gym visits by **51%** at first, but the advantage faded after a holiday broke the routine ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/researchers-used-hunger-games-encourage-healthier-choices/)). It is worth offering as an optional "pair it with" slot, with a fresh-start prompt after disruptions. Tiny Habits and identity-based framing ("I'm someone who…") are popular, but this research found no RCT for either, so they belong as optional framing, not core mechanics.

Self-monitoring reliably works. Harkin's meta-analysis of **138 studies (N=19,951)** found that prompting progress monitoring improved goal attainment by **d = 0.40**, and that the effect was larger when progress was physically recorded or reported to someone ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000025.pdf)). Reminders are more complicated. A review of 115 habit apps found that most focused on tracking and reminders, and that reminders supported repetition but **hindered** the shift to automaticity. Event-based cues combined with implementation intentions did better ([Stawarz, Cox & Blandford 2015](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2702123.2702230)). For an ADHD user this is a real trade-off rather than a rule. Barkley argues that external scaffolds have to be permanent for ADHD ([Barkley fact sheet](https://www.russellbarkley.org/factsheets/ADHD_EF_and_SR.pdf)). Pippin should therefore anchor nudges to context (the anchor routine's time or place) and should not try to wean users off them.

On flexibility and streaks, three findings settle Pippin's design. Paying people to exercise in a fixed, self-chosen daily window produced **less** gym attendance than paying for visits at any time, and did not build stronger habits ([Beshears et al. 2021](https://katherinemilkman.squarespace.com/s/creating-exercise-habits.pdf)). Highlighting an intact streak increases engagement, but a broken streak is especially demotivating, because the user has failed both the behaviour and the streak, and people who break streaks are more likely to leave. The authors advise never notifying people about broken streaks and note that "what counts as a streak is malleable" ([Silverman, Barasch & Small, JCR 2023](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/49/6/1095/6623414); [Phys.org interview](https://phys.org/news/2024-03-streaks.html)). Temporal landmarks such as a new week, month or birthday reliably restart goal pursuit, because they put past failures in "a previous self" ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Dai-Milkman-Riis_FreshStartEffect_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)). The emergency-reserve research supports Pippin's existing pause and repair features specifically. Goals with "slack with a cost", meaning a few skip days the user would rather not spend, were both preferred and produced more persistence, including after a sub-goal failure ([Sharif & Shu 2017](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Benefits-of-Emergency-Reserves:-Greater-and-for-Sharif-Shu/cc22c417ab7f36fee5e364e9a4cda2e7078af540); [Sharif & Shu 2019](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf)). A small, limited allowance of repairs therefore beats unlimited free skips.

The best progress visual already exists in open source. Loop Habit Tracker's score uses exponential smoothing with a constant of about 0.052. A perfect record reaches about 80% after one month, 96% after two and 99% after three, and "a few missed days after a long streak will not completely destroy your progress" ([uhabits on GitHub](https://github.com/iSoron/uhabits); [Loop discussion #689](https://github.com/iSoron/uhabits/discussions/689)). The constant came from a search snippet, so check it against the source before relying on it. This score matches Lally's curve far better than a day count, and it can drive the pet's growth directly. There are clear evidence gaps here. This research found **no habit-formation study in adults with ADHD and no trial comparing streak and no-streak designs**. The ADHD-specific choices are extrapolations from general habit science plus clinical consensus: externalise memory, make time visible, reward immediately, start tiny and keep the active list short.

## Sobriety apps sell a clock, but the evidence sits in tracking, substitution and problem solving

The sobriety-app market agrees on a core feature set. I Am Sober has a start-date clock with automatic milestones, money, time and calorie savings, a morning pledge with an evening review, and a reorderable "reasons" list shown during the review. Its reset keeps "all your previous start dates, milestone history, reasons, savings data, and photos", and community access is gated behind **five completed pledges** ([I Am Sober FAQ](https://iamsober.com/en/site/faq)). Nomo offers unlimited private clocks, "Refocus" exercises for moments of temptation, and accountability partners who can be told when someone is tempted ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/nomo-sobriety-clocks/id566975787)). The UK's evidence-led apps look different. Try Dry, the free Dry January app from Alcohol Change UK, supports cutting down as well as stopping. It tracks units, calories and money, and adds badges, charts, daily reminders and email coaching ([Alcohol Change UK](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/help-and-support/managing-your-drinking/dry-january/get-involved/the-dry-january-app)). UCL's Drink Less is built around goals, drink logging, mood and sleep recording, feedback and action plans ([MedicalXpress/UCL](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2024-03-app-people-alcohol-intake.html)).

The outcome evidence is real but small, and it points away from the parts of these apps that get the most marketing attention. A Cochrane review of 57 studies found that users of digital interventions drank **about 23 g less alcohol per week**, roughly 3 UK units, than people with minimal or no intervention ([Kaner et al. 2017](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD011479.pub2/full)). A meta-regression of 41 of those trials found three behaviour-change techniques associated with larger effects. **Behaviour substitution** was linked to a 95 g/week reduction, but this rests on only four trials, three of them from one group. **Problem solving** was linked to 46 g/week and a **credible source** to 32 g/week. Feedback on behaviour and social comparison, the most common techniques, showed no independent effect, and adding more techniques did not help ([Garnett et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6361280)). The flagship UK trial should temper expectations. Drink Less (n=5,602) was compared against the NHS alcohol webpage, and the pre-registered conservative analysis found a **non-significant 0.98 units/week** extra reduction. UCL's press framing of "about 2 units" relies on less conservative analyses ([SSRN preprint](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4495135); [MedicalXpress](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2024-03-app-people-alcohol-intake.html)). Self-monitoring and feedback took **85% of screen views**, and tracking looks like the working mechanism ([npj Digital Medicine 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01169-7)). For a user who has already decided to stop, Pippin is not trying to persuade them. Its job is to sustain a decision through a year of celebrations, work stress and family evenings, and to make a lapse survivable.

Temporary-abstinence challenges have encouraging but uncontrolled evidence. A 2025 scoping review of 16 studies, most of them observational and 10 from the UK, found that about two-thirds of registrants finish the month. At 1–6 months they report lower drinking and AUDIT scores, higher drink-refusal self-efficacy and better wellbeing. **Rebound was uncommon: 8% of successful abstainers versus 15% of non-completers** reported getting drunk more often at six months. Participants skew towards higher-income, educated, lighter drinkers, and rising participation has not shifted drinking across the population ([Strowger et al. 2025](https://academic.oup.com/alcalc/article/60/5/agaf057/8249062)). In a prospective study of moderate-to-heavy drinkers, one month off cut insulin resistance by about **26%** and systolic blood pressure by **6.6%**, with no change in people who kept drinking. The study was observational and self-selected ([Mehta et al. 2018](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10048446/1/e020673.full.pdf)). Alcohol Change UK says that using its app plus the daily email journey **doubles** the chance of a fully dry month ([Alcohol Change UK](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/help-and-support/managing-your-drinking/dry-january/get-involved/the-dry-january-app)). This research found **no studies of year-long challenges**. Evidence ends about six months after a one-month challenge, so a year-long challenge goes beyond what has been studied, and Pippin should measure the user's progress rather than assume it.

User complaints show what an open-source release can fix cheaply. I Am Sober reviewers report losing their history because backup sits behind the paywall, unexpected resets, day counts that are off by one, and a community feed with harmful comments, including encouragement to self-harm and minors discussing self-harm ([JustUseApp reviews](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/672904239/i-am-sober/reviews); [App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/i-am-sober/id672904239?see-all=reviews)). On r/stopdrinking, the culture runs on IWNDWYT ("I will not drink with you today"), a daily pledge whose "with you" is the point ([AddictionHelp](https://www.addictionhelp.com/alcohol/iwndwyt/)). Pippin can match the loved parts (pledge, review, reasons, milestones) and remove the hated ones (paywalled backup, fragile counters, open communities) at no cost.

## A drink is information: designing the counter around the abstinence violation effect

Relapse prevention distinguishes a **lapse**, a single return to drinking, from a **relapse**, a return to the old pattern. What connects the two is often the abstinence violation effect: "the self-blame, guilt, and loss of perceived control that individuals often experience after the violation of self-imposed rules" ([Larimer, Palmer & Marlatt](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6760427/)). It is triggered by the thought "that the lapse proves they are a failure and will never be capable of permanent abstinence" ([Witkiewitz et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5441879/)). How someone explains a lapse matters. Internal, stable, global explanations ("I'm weak") predict progression to relapse, while situational ones ("that dinner, that stress, no plan") do not ([Walton et al. 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7942249/)). Witkiewitz and Marlatt's dynamic model splits risk into slow-moving **tonic** factors (baseline craving, self-efficacy, coping skills, support) and in-the-moment **phasic** triggers ([dynamic model figure](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Dynamic-Model-of-Relapse_fig1_8557471)). That split maps directly onto product structure. Tonic features are the reasons list, the weekly review, sleep and medication logging, and support contacts. Phasic features are the urge button, if-then plans and a delay timer.

A hard reset to zero is the abstinence violation effect expressed as a screen. Pippin should show **two numbers with unequal prominence**. The headline is "alcohol-free days this year" (for example, "212 of 219 days"), which a drink lowers by one day and never wipes. Secondary and opt-in is "days since last drink", the run that Pippin's optional forgiving-streak setting already supports. A year calendar shows a lapse as one coloured cell among hundreds rather than a blank slate. The phrase "AF days out of 365" makes the arithmetic explicit: one drink costs one day, not the year. The "Dry January" non-completer data points the same way, since people who slipped still benefited and the bad outcome was abandonment ([Strowger et al. 2025](https://academic.oup.com/alcalc/article/60/5/agaf057/8249062)). Newer apps already work like this. SoberStack promises "progress isn't erased by a slip", and Sober Tracker awards a "Phoenix" badge for coming back ([SoberStack](https://soberstack.app/); [Sober Tracker](https://sober-tracker.com/)). The evidence has limits. **No study directly compares streak-reset and cumulative displays in alcohol apps**, so this design rests on relapse-prevention theory plus convergent app practice, not on a head-to-head trial.

How Pippin's existing streak mechanics apply to sobriety needs a deliberate decision, because they do not transfer as they are. Pause and repair suit habits, where the evidence on emergency reserves applies and a skipped walk is morally neutral. For alcohol, **a repair token that erases a drinking day would corrupt the self-monitoring that carries the evidence**. It could also act as a pre-paid licence ("I've got a repair, so tonight's fine"). That second risk is an inference, not a tested finding, but it is serious enough to design around. The sobriety tracker needs no repair mechanic because nothing in it breaks: the cumulative count is monotonic and the run is secondary. What Pippin should reward is **honest logging of a lapse**. The pet responds warmly to a logged slip and never sickens or shrinks. A "came back" marker records the return, so the user's most valuable act after a drink is to open the app, not avoid it.

The lapse flow itself should use the techniques with the best evidence. Logging a drink opens a short debrief (when, where, with whom, feeling, units). It asks a situational question ("what was going on?") rather than a global one, and it ends with a "next 24 hours" plan and a new if-then plan for that situation. That combines problem solving, a technique linked to effect in the meta-regression, with the attribution research. If-then plans for alcohol work, but they work modestly and fade. A 2023 meta-analysis found implementation intentions reduce weekly drinking (**d = 0.14**) but not heavy episodic drinking. An earlier analysis found d = 0.43 within one month and a non-significant 0.07 after that ([Cooke et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10087331/)). Over a year, Pippin should **prompt the user to refresh their plans monthly and before every known high-risk event**, not set them once. For the in-the-moment urge, mindfulness-based relapse prevention has the strongest trial support. In Bowen's RCT of 286 people, both MBRP and cognitive-behavioural relapse prevention beat usual care at six months, and MBRP reduced heavy drinking further at twelve ([Bowen et al. 2014](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/1839290)). Urge surfing is MBRP's core skill: watching a craving rise and fall like a wave without acting on it ([PMC5441879](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5441879/)). It fits behind an "I'm having an urge" button as a 5–15-minute guided timer that logs intensity before and after, and so also produces self-monitoring data. The best smoking-app RCT points the same way. iCanQuit, which is built on acceptance of urges, beat a guideline-based app on 12-month abstinence (**28.2% vs 21.1%**) ([Fred Hutch](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/releases/2020/09/fred-hutch-led-clinical-trial-shows-new-smartphone-app-helps-smokers-quit.html)). HALT and "delay, distract, decide" are common in mutual-aid culture, but this research found no trial evidence for either. They can appear as prompts, not as headline techniques.

Alcohol-free drinks are a two-sided tool and should be a user setting with honest copy. A systematic review found that no/low drinks raised craving in people with alcohol use disorder in proportion to dependence severity ([Caballeria et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/19/3925)). UK women in recovery described them as both a weaning aid and a relapse trigger ([Davey et al. 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/dar.13766)). Images of social drinking raised craving even when the drinks shown were non-alcoholic ([ScienceDirect 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306460324001746)). The Institute of Alcohol Studies concludes that no/lo drinks reduce harm only when they actually replace alcohol ([IAS 2026](https://www.ias.org.uk/2026/06/18/no-lo-drinks-and-alcohol-harm-reduction-a-targeted-tool-not-a-population-level-solution/)). This is also where the habit tracker and the sobriety tracker should connect. Behaviour substitution is the technique with the largest estimated effect, so the sobriety setup should offer to create "instead" habits in the habit tracker, such as an evening walk, a specific alcohol-free drink or a 6pm bath. The two features then reinforce each other instead of competing for attention.

The first big test of a challenge is often a celebration near its start. No trial covers sober celebrations, so an event-preparation flow has to be built from the if-then evidence. It would cover a pre-committed plan, a drink in hand early, an ally who knows, an exit time, a short refusal script ("I'm doing a year off; I'll have an X") and a check-in the next morning. Dry January completers report more confidence in refusing drinks at six months ([Alcohol Change UK evidence](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/help-and-support/managing-your-drinking/dry-january/about-dry-january/the-evidence-behind-the-dry-january-challenge-benefits-and-long-term-impact)). A short drink-refusal self-efficacy check every month or so would therefore track the outcome that most plausibly predicts the year holding, which a day count does not.

## For a medicated adult with ADHD, the alcohol-free year and the medication protect each other

ADHD is a significant risk factor for alcohol problems. Childhood ADHD predicts alcohol use disorder with odds ratios of about **1.35–2.15** across meta-analyses ([Lee et al. 2011](http://www.kathrynhumphreys.com/uploads/4/3/0/6/43065295/lee_2011_metaanalysis.pdf); [Charach et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21156266/); [Groenman et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S089085671730206X)). About half of adults aged 20–39 with ADHD have had a substance use disorder, most often alcohol ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/924775)). When people in substance-use treatment are screened, about **21–23%** have ADHD ([CNS Drugs 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40263-025-01168-6)). Two mechanisms matter for the design. Emotional dysregulation is a significant feature of adult ADHD ([PMC9568011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9568011/)), so stress and conflict are likely phasic triggers. Heavy drinkers with poorer response inhibition show more cue-elicited craving ([UCLA cue-reactivity study](https://addictions.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/160/2018/01/AJDAA-The-Effect-Of-Alcohol-Priming-On-Neural-Markers-Of-Alcohol-Cue-Reactivity.pdf)), so planning ahead to avoid cues will serve an ADHD user better than relying on in-the-moment willpower.

Medication is protective and should be treated as part of the plan, not a separate topic. In claims data on about 3 million US patients with ADHD, medicated periods were associated with **35% (men) and 31% (women) lower odds** of substance-related events ([Quinn et al. 2017](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.2017.16060686)). Swedish registers show a similar reduction of about 31% with long-term stimulant treatment ([Chang et al. 2014](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jcpp.12164)), and stimulants do not raise later substance use ([JAMA Psychiatry 2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2806881)). The interaction runs the other way too. Combining lisdexamfetamine with alcohol may increase cardiovascular side effects such as heart rate and blood pressure, and patients are advised to avoid or limit alcohol ([Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/article/adhd-medication-alcohol.html); [BMC Psychiatry review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4628434/)). NHS shared-care guidance for adults says to avoid alcohol during ADHD treatment ([NCL shared care guideline](https://nclhealthandcare.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/SCG_ADHD_adults.pdf)). Pippin can reasonably pre-fill "it's kinder to my medication and my heart" as a suggested reason. A private, optional medication tick can sit next to the sobriety check-in as a tonic protective factor. It must never advise on dose or timing. One plausible risk is unsourced and should be treated as a hypothesis to test on the user's own data: the medication wears off in the late afternoon and evening, which is when drinking usually happens. If their logs show urges clustering then, the evening anchor becomes the most important nudge of the day.

## Safety gates differ by substance, and the counter must change with them

### Alcohol: screen before day one, and keep emergency guidance close

Pippin should use the UK versions of AUDIT-C and AUDIT, which ask about UK units, with their official thresholds ([gov.uk AUDIT-C](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1113177/Alcohol-use-disorders-identification-test-for-consumption-AUDIT-C_for-print.pdf); [gov.uk AUDIT](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6357a7af8fa8f557d85b7c44/Alcohol-use-disorders-identification-test-AUDIT_for-print.pdf)). AUDIT-C is the first three AUDIT questions: frequency, units on a typical drinking day, and how often the user has 6+ units (women) or 8+ units (men) in one session. It scores 0–12. The full AUDIT adds questions on loss of control, failed obligations, morning drinking, guilt, blackouts, injury and others' concern, and scores 0–40. Drinkaware and the NHS publish both for self-completion ([Drinkaware](https://www.drinkaware.co.uk/facts/information-about-alcohol/alcohol-and-the-facts/understanding-the-alcohol-use-disorders-identification-test)). Computerised self-report also tends to bring out more honest answers about sensitive behaviour than paper does ([Gnambs & Kaspar 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-014-0533-4)).

| Score | AUDIT-C (0–12) | Full AUDIT (0–40) | What Pippin does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low risk | 0–4 | 0–7 | Start the challenge; show the withdrawal-signs card once |
| Increasing risk | 5–7 (positive screen) | 8–15 | AUDIT-C ≥5 → offer the full AUDIT; brief-advice content |
| Higher risk | 8–10 | 16–19 | Strongly suggest a GP or local alcohol service conversation before starting; challenge still allowed |
| Possible dependence | 11–12 | **20+** | **Blocking but respectful screen:** "Don't stop suddenly on your own; speak to your GP first." NICE CG115 advises considering assisted withdrawal at AUDIT ≥20 |

NICE grades severity with the SADQ. A score of 15 or less usually needs no assisted withdrawal. A score of 15–30 usually needs assisted withdrawal, typically in the community, and above 30 typically needs inpatient care ([NICE CG115](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg115/resources/alcoholuse-disorders-diagnosis-assessment-and-management-of-harmful-drinking-highrisk-drinking-and-alcohol-dependence-pdf-35109391116229)). Pippin should not administer the SADQ. That assessment belongs to the GP. The pre-start gate should fire on **any** of the following, whatever the score: an AUDIT of 20 or more, any morning drinking (item 6 above zero), daily drinking, or any past withdrawal symptoms, fits, hallucinations or confusion after stopping. The NHS warns that for someone physically dependent, "stopping overnight could be harmful". It lists anxiety after waking, sweating and mild tremors, morning nausea or retching, and vomiting as withdrawal symptoms that may need medicine. It says to **call 999 for hallucinations, severe tremors, or seizures or fits** ([NHS alcohol support](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/alcohol-advice/alcohol-support/)). The danger window is short and predictable. Symptoms peak at 24–48 hours. Seizures occur in 1–15% of people in withdrawal, usually 12–36 hours after the last drink. Delirium tremens usually begins at 48–72 hours, can start anywhere from day 1 to day 5, and carries up to 15–20% mortality untreated, falling to 0–1% with treatment ([Patient.info](https://patient.info/doctor/mental-health/acute-alcohol-withdrawal-and-delirium-tremens)). Pippin should therefore keep a one-tap "warning signs" card on every screen for **days 0–5**, and must never suggest that willpower can manage physical withdrawal. For the likely persona, a moderate AUDIT score with no withdrawal history, this gate is a safety net seen once. For an unknown public user it may be the most important screen in the app. NICE CG115 dates from 2011, so check for an update before shipping.

Unit maths should use the NHS formula, **ABV (%) × volume (ml) ÷ 1,000**, with presets such as a 175 ml glass of 12% wine = 2.1 units and a pint of 5.2% beer = 3 units. The context line is the UK Chief Medical Officers' guidance of no more than **14 units a week**, spread over three or more days ([NHS units](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/alcohol-advice/calculating-alcohol-units/)). Re-running AUDIT-C at 3, 6 and 12 months gives the year an outcome measure that is clinically meaningful in a way a day count is not.

### Other substances and behaviours: per-category rules for the public release

A public release has to stop treating "quit X" as a single flow. For some categories, the generic "start your clock today" can cause harm.

| Category | Gate before setting a goal | Lapse response | Category-specific rules | Key UK contact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alcohol | AUDIT-C/AUDIT plus withdrawal questions; hard stop at ≥20 or any withdrawal history | Debrief, next-24h plan | Units, not "drinks"; no/lo drinks as a setting | Drinkline 0300 123 1110 |

### Design principles

The first principle is that **the headline number never goes down**. Cumulative AF days, a habit-strength score that decays gently, "weeks on target" and the pet's growth all move in one direction, or nearly so. Anything that can fall to zero is secondary, opt-in and labelled as a run, not a record. The second is that **honest logging is the rewarded act**. After a lapse, the pet and coach respond to *opening the app and logging*, with warmth and a plan, because the bad outcome in every dataset is abandonment, not the drink. The third is that **Pippin's forgiving streaks keep their scoped role and gain one exception**. They stay optional, cosmetic and silent when broken for habits, as the earlier coach blueprint settled. In the sobriety tracker there is no repair or pause, because a counter that cannot break needs no repair, and a token that erases a drink would undermine the data the evidence depends on. The fourth is that **language is situational, never global**: "a drink day", "a lapse", "what was going on?", never "relapse", "failed", "clean" or "addict" unless the user chooses those words. The fifth is that **plans decay and must be refreshed**. If-then plans fade after a month, so they are re-made monthly and before every known high-risk event. The sixth is **permanent scaffolding**, as Barkley prescribes for ADHD. Nudges, widgets and check-ins are never withdrawn to "build independence". The seventh is that **the coach never uses a counter as leverage**. "Don't lose your 47 days" is the abstinence violation effect in the coach's own voice. The eighth is that **sobriety data stays private by default**: local storage, free export and backup, an app lock, a neutral icon, notification text that never names the substance, no analytics on which category is tracked, and per-category hiding on shared family devices. These are inferences, not sourced findings, but they fix the most common complaints about commercial apps.

### Safety boundaries

| Boundary | Rule |
|---|---|
| Pre-start alcohol gate | AUDIT-C at onboarding; ≥5 → full AUDIT; 16–19 → strongly suggest a GP conversation; **≥20, any morning drinking, daily drinking, or any past shakes, sweats, fits, hallucinations or confusion when stopping → "Don't stop suddenly on your own. Speak to your GP or local alcohol service first; they can help you stop safely."** The user can continue only after acknowledging this, and the app never claims to manage withdrawal |
| Emergency signs (days 0–5, one tap from every screen) | "If you or someone else has a fit, sees or hears things that aren't there, has severe shaking, or becomes confused after stopping drinking, **call 999**." Mild morning anxiety, sweats, tremor or nausea → contact your GP or NHS 111 today |
| Deterministic crisis layer | Extend the existing out-of-model crisis screen with withdrawal-emergency phrases (fits, seeing things, can't stop shaking) and lapse-despair phrases ("what's the point", "I've ruined it"). It shows fixed wording and stays in the conversation rather than closing it, consistent with the earlier coach design |
| Lapse vs relapse escalation | One lapse → debrief only. A pattern (for example, several drinking days in a fortnight, a return to the baseline pattern, or drinking to cope with distress) → a gentle suggestion of a GP, local service, AA or SMART Recovery, without pressure. These thresholds are design judgements, not sourced cut-offs. Any suicidal content goes to the crisis layer immediately |
| Medication | Log adherence privately; suggest a prescriber review for side effects or missed doses; never advise on dose, timing or stopping; state that alcohol plus lisdexamfetamine may raise heart rate and blood pressure |
| Per-category hard rules | No abrupt-quit flow for benzodiazepines, GHB/GBL or dependent alcohol; an overdose card on every opioid or depressant lapse; psychosis signs with cannabis escalate to GP/111/999; no calorie, weight or restriction tracking in binge-eating mode; gambling losses or debt trigger the helpline |
| No clinical content | No taper schedules, doses, drug-checking advice or diagnosis. AUDIT is described as "a screen, not a diagnosis" |
| Community | None in Pippin. If ever added to the public release: 18+, gated behind a number of completed pledges, and moderated |
| Intended purpose (public release) | "Self-help tracking and coaching for changing habits and substance use; not a treatment, not for managing withdrawal or dependence." This keeps Pippin in NICE's Tier B and outside MHRA device scope, per the earlier coach research |

### Notification design

Pippin's notifications should carry content, follow context and stay discreet. The daily rhythm is a short **morning pledge** and an **evening check-in** timed to the user's actual risk window, which early logs will reveal. This mirrors the I Am Sober pledge-and-review loop and the Dry January email journey that about 70% of subscribers read in full ([Strowger et al. 2025](https://academic.oup.com/alcalc/article/60/5/agaf057/8249062)). Each nudge should include something useful: one reason from their list, the relevant if-then plan, or a one-line skill. A bare "check in!" adds nothing. Habit nudges fire at the anchor routine rather than at an arbitrary time ([Stawarz et al. 2015](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2702123.2702230)). **Anticipatory nudges** fire before known high-risk moments, such as a Friday evening, a calendar event tagged with drinks, or the run-up to a big party, and prompt a plan refresh because if-then effects fade. **Fresh-start nudges** go out on Mondays, on the 1st of the month and after a quiet spell ("new week; want the two-minute version?") ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Dai-Milkman-Riis_FreshStartEffect_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)). Milestones are celebrated in the app, not pushed as pressure.

Several notification types should never be sent: a broken-streak alert ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2024-03-streaks.html)), a "you missed X days" count, an escalating series after a lapse, or any lock-screen text that names alcohol, a substance or a behaviour. After a logged lapse Pippin sends **at most one** warm follow-up the next morning ("yesterday's logged; today's plan is ready when you are") and then returns to the normal rhythm. For ADHD users, being able to choose *more persistent* alerts is legitimate; the earlier coach research found that some ADHD users ask for longer, stickier notifications. The default cap should still be low (a judgement call; something like three or four sobriety-and-habit pushes a day in total), with a single "quiet today" switch that the evening check-in respects.

### What not to build

| Do not build | Why |
|---|---|
| A reset-to-zero button or a zero shown as the headline after a drink | Turns a lapse into the abstinence violation effect on screen |
| Repair tokens, pauses or "freezes" on the sobriety counter | Corrupts the self-monitoring data and risks acting as a pre-paid licence (inference); nothing in the design needs repairing |
| Pet sickness, decay, HP loss or "sad pet" states after a lapse or miss | Punishment mechanics drive avoidance and quitting |
| Broken-streak or missed-day notifications | Silverman et al.: broken streaks predict leaving |
| An open community feed or chat | I Am Sober's documented self-harm content and minors; moderation burden |
| Paywalled or cloud-only backup | The single most common cause of lost sobriety history |
| Withdrawal management, taper schedules, dosing or drug-checking advice | Clinical territory; abrupt or wrong advice can be fatal |
| A generic "quit today" flow for benzodiazepines, GHB/GBL or dependent alcohol | Seizure and delirium risk |
| A streak-reset message for opioids | Lost tolerance makes the first use after abstinence the riskiest |
| Calorie, weight or "clean day" tracking in binge-eating mode | Restriction drives binges; links to eating-disorder symptoms |
| Moral, purity or "clean/dirty" language, and "addict" unless the user chooses it | Moral incongruence and shame worsen outcomes |
| Social comparison and normative leaderboards | No independent effect in the meta-regression; feeds rejection sensitivity |
| Piling on behaviour-change techniques or content libraries | More techniques did not mean more effect; ADHD users complete about one module |
| Health claims with precise recovery timelines beyond what NHS/BMJ sources support | Overclaiming undermines the "credible source" effect |
| The coach citing counters, streaks or money saved as pressure | Turns rewards into the shame mechanic Pippin's scoped streak decision excludes |
| Covert notifications to a partner or anyone else | Accountability sharing must be opt-in and agreed in advance |

## Conclusion

The research turns "how should a sobriety counter look?" into a question about which number the user sees on the morning after a drink. Every strand of evidence — Marlatt's lapse model, the streak-abandonment data, the Dry January non-completers who still benefited, Lally's harmless missed day — says that number decides whether a slip stays a slip. That makes the cumulative, never-falling counter the main safety feature of the sobriety tracker, in the same way the deterministic crisis screen is the main safety feature of the coach. The other notable result is how little the visible furniture of sobriety apps does. Clocks, badges and savings counters are well liked but have no demonstrated effect. The ingredients with evidence are quieter ones: honest daily tracking, an "instead" behaviour, a problem-solving debrief and refreshed if-then plans. Pippin's habit tracker and sobriety tracker should therefore be one system, where replacement habits are the substitution technique and lapse debriefs feed the plans.

Three things remain unknown and are worth measuring on Pippin's first user rather than assuming. No study has followed a one-year alcohol-free challenge. No trial has compared cumulative and reset counters. No research has tested habit formation in medicated adults with ADHD. A single user's year can produce honest single-case data on all three: AUDIT-C at 3, 6 and 12 months, monthly drink-refusal confidence, the timing of urges against their medication cycle, and whether lapses stay single. That data matters more than any feature for deciding what the public release keeps.
